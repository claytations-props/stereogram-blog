Research Notes

Crawford Notch, where the house outlived the family

A narrow gap in the White Mountains was opened three times, by a horse trail, by inns and a footpath, and by a railroad shelf blasted into a cliff, and each opening cost its builders something different.

The gap that had to be worked

Crawford Notch is a narrow pass through the White Mountains, with the Saco River running down its floor and steep walls on both sides. The same road could wash out in a night, and the same walls could come down on a house.

The first man in the story to try the gap for a living was Timothy Nash, a Lancaster hunter. Nash tracked a moose over Cherry Mountain and worked down through the notch with his friend Benjamin Sawyer and a farm horse. Governor John Wentworth doubted a road could be built there, so he offered Nash land at the head of the notch if he could get a horse through from Lancaster and build a road to it from the east. Nash and Sawyer got the horse through, lowering the animal over boulders with ropes where the ground gave them no other choice. The road that followed was at first little more than a trail.

Native people, including Abenaki travellers, had used routes through the notch for centuries. The horse trek matters because it turned an existing route into a promised road with land attached, not because it invented the pass.

A night's lodging and a walk up a mountain

Abel Crawford and his wife Hannah Rosebrook settled first near Fabyans, then moved south into the notch at Hart's Location, where Hannah Rosebrook's father, Eleazar Rosebrook, joined them. Both families kept inns on the notch road. Abel Crawford kept a family inn called the Mount Crawford House. Ethan Allen Crawford, Abel Crawford's son, took over the inn Eleazar Rosebrook had run.

He once met a wagon bogged down on the road, fitted sled runners in place of its wheels, got the wagon through to Bethlehem, swapped the runners back to wheels, and took a dollar for his trouble.

Travellers asked Ethan Allen Crawford and Abel Crawford to guide them up Mount Washington, a trackless summit. The two men cut a footpath from the head of the notch at Saco Lake toward the summit and advertised it in newspapers. Later, Thomas Jefferson Crawford, Ethan Allen Crawford's son, improved it into a bridle path for riders. Samuel C. Eastman warned readers in his guidebook: "The distance to the Summit is, according to the guide board at the entrance to the path, only seven miles, but if you have a pleasant day you will be by no means sorry to find it nine, which is commonly supposed to be nearer the truth."

Ethan Allen Crawford's wife, Lucy Howe Crawford, wrote the White Mountains history that became the main account of her husband's life. Her book shows the other side of the ledger: he inherited his grandfather Eleazar Rosebrook's farm with a mortgage of three thousand dollars, an uninsured tavern burned, he went to jail briefly for debts he could not pay, and he died essentially broke. The family gave the notch its name, cut its path, and kept its inns, and the work did not make them rich.

The night the slide passed the house

Samuel Willey Jr. and his wife Polly Willey moved into a house in the notch the fall before the slide, with their five children and two hired men, to run it as an inn. The house was the Old Notch House, built a generation earlier, under the mountain now called Mount Willey. The Willey family enlarged it, added stables and a barn, and called it the Willey House Inn and Tavern. Most of the people who stopped were farmers passing through toward Portland, not the sightseers the family had hoped for.

One summer month brought a slide across the Saco River, on the far side of the valley from the house. Samuel Willey Jr. built a shelter above the house where his family could run if a slide threatened their side.

Then came a long drought, and a storm that broke over the notch on the night of August 28. Rain fell in torrents through the night and the Saco River rose far above its banks. The Willey family left the house in the dark. Searchers found the house untouched, the barn crushed, two horses dead under the ruins, beds left hurriedly, clothes scattered, and a Bible on the table, with a dog howling in the empty rooms. The slide had split on a rock ledge behind the house and passed on both sides, rejoining in front of it, so stones came within a few feet of the walls while the house itself stood. The family was found outside it: Samuel Willey Jr. and Polly Willey, two of their children, and both hired men, crushed by earth and stone and timber. Three children were never found. No one who found the house could say which way the family had run in the dark. Whether they ran for the shelter above the house or uphill from the rising river, the slide caught them outside the one building it spared.

Nathaniel Hawthorne turned the Willey story into "The Ambitious Guest," about a family in a cottage in the Notch and a traveller who stops for the night. The tale is fiction, and its people are not the Willey family, but his picture of the innkeeping life is exact about the work: "The stage-coach always drew up before the door of the cottage." His traveller tells the family, "I meant to have been at Ethan Crawford's to-night; but a pedestrian lingers along such a road as this." His landlord jokes about the mountain above the house: "The old mountain has thrown a stone at us, for fear we should forget him." Hawthorne and the painter Thomas Cole drew sightseers to the notch in numbers the Willey family had hoped for and never lived to serve. After the tragedy, the Willey House itself was enlarged and run as an inn. News of the slide spread through newspapers and guidebooks, drawing tourists to the site. The horror became the advertisement.

A shelf cut into the cliff

A generation after the slide, the notch got its third opening, built with drills and black powder. Samuel Anderson and his brother John Anderson, sons of a Portland lawyer who had run the port, pushed the Portland and Ogdensburg Railroad through the mountains toward an ice-free port. Samuel Anderson served as president of the company. John Anderson served as chief engineer and supervisor of the work through the notch, a route other engineers had called impossible.

The work season ran short, from late spring to early autumn, with men in bunkhouses at Sawyer's River and Bartlett. They drilled by hand, in teams of three: one man turned a star-pronged drill while a man on either side swung a sledgehammer in turn, boring about three feet into granite before black powder went into the hole. A blacksmith forge ran fourteen hours a day sharpening the drills. For most of the ten miles between Sawyer's River Station and Crawford Notch Station, the crew had to make a shelf on the west side of the notch, blasting ledges and filling gorges as they went.

The Frankenstein Trestle, named for Godfrey Frankenstein, a painter who worked in the White Mountains, carried the track five hundred feet across a gulf, eighty feet above the rocks, on iron later replaced with steel. The Willey Brook Bridge, farther along the line, stood one hundred feet high and four hundred feet long. The grade ran as steep as one hundred and sixteen feet to the mile for nine miles, and the line rose more than thirteen hundred feet between North Conway and Crawford Station over eighteen and a half miles. When the first train on the mountain route reached Fabyan Station, the line from Portland through the notch was open, and hotel business at the head of the notch jumped.

The Canadian grain trade the Anderson brothers had counted on never came in the volume they needed, because an older rival line already held much of that traffic. The line reached only as far as St. Johnsbury, Vermont, never to Ogdensburg, and the company went bankrupt in the 1880s. The Maine Central Railroad leased the line, then bought it outright. The shelf, the trestle, and the grade outlasted the company that paid for them.

What kept standing

Thomas Jefferson Crawford had started a hotel at the head of the notch, the Crawford House, and sold it unfinished when his money ran out. Ebenezer Eastman of Littleton bought it, finished it, and expanded it. Asa Barron's company ran it for decades after buying it. The Crawford family did not run the Crawford House, for all that the notch and the hotel carry their name. The hotel closed in the 1970s, burned while empty, and the Appalachian Mountain Club Highland Center now stands on its site.

The state park holds almost six thousand acres, with a visitor area at the Willey House site. The Crawford Path still runs from the notch to Mount Washington's summit as part of a longer trail. The railroad shelf still cuts through rock the Anderson crews blasted. Stand at the marked Willey site: a house stood here, a slide passed around it, a family died outside it, and strangers kept coming to see where that happened. The notch did not reward the people who opened it. It kept their work.

Appendix

Timeline

  • 1771 — Timothy Nash, a Lancaster hunter tracking a moose over Cherry Mountain, works through the notch with Benjamin Sawyer and a farm horse, reporting the route to Governor John Wentworth. The route was already used by Native people, including Abenaki travellers.
  • 1775 — Road, at first little more than a trail, opened.
  • Early 1790s — Abel Crawford, wife Hannah Rosebrook, and family settle first near Fabyans, then at Hart's Location; Eleazar Rosebrook joins them; both families run inns. NH Parks gives 1790 at Fabyans; AMC gives by 1791, Hart's Location 1792.
  • 1793 — Old Notch House built, later the Willey House.
  • 1803 — Tenth New Hampshire Turnpike chartered.
  • 1819 — Abel Crawford and Ethan Allen Crawford cut the Crawford Path from the head of the notch at Saco Lake toward Mount Washington, then advertise it in newspapers.
  • 1820 — Party guided by Ethan Allen Crawford names Monroe, Jefferson, Adams, Madison, Franklin, and Mount Pleasant.
  • Fall 1825 — Samuel Willey Jr., wife Polly Willey, five children, and two hired men move into the Old Notch House and run it as an inn.
  • June 1826 — Slide across the Saco River frightens the Willey family; Samuel Willey Jr. builds a shelter above the house.
  • Night of August 28, 1826 — Violent storm after drought; slide kills Samuel Willey Jr., Polly Willey, five children, and two hired men, nine people; house stands; barn crushed; bodies of the parents, two children, and both hired men found nearby; three children never found. Saco River rise: 20 feet (NH Parks, AMC) and 16 feet above usual height at daybreak (Perley). Gravestone ages: Samuel Willey 38, Polly S. Willey 35, Eliza A. Willey 12, Jeremiah S. Willey 11, Martha G. Willey 10, Elbridge G. Willey 7, Sally Willey 3.
  • 1835 — Hawthorne publishes "The Ambitious Guest," based on the Willey story.
  • 1839 — Thomas Cole paints "A View of the Pass Called the Notch of the White Mountains."
  • 1839–1840 — Thomas Jefferson Crawford improves the Crawford Path to a bridle path; horses on the trail by 1840.
  • 1846 — Lucy Howe Crawford publishes "The History of the White Mountains from the First Settlement of Upper Coos and Pequaket"; Ethan Allen Crawford dies June 22, 1846, essentially broke.
  • 1850 — Thomas Jefferson Crawford starts building the hotel then known as the Crawford House; sells it unfinished.
  • 1858 — Samuel C. Eastman publishes "The White Mountain Guide Book."
  • 1859 — Crawford House burns, is rebuilt and opened for the 1859 season.
  • 1867 — Portland and Ogdensburg Railroad chartered in Maine.
  • 1869 — Portland and Ogdensburg Railroad chartered in New Hampshire.
  • 1870–1908 — Asa Barron's company operates the Crawford House from 1870.
  • 1871 — Rails reach North Conway.
  • 1874 — Rails reach Bartlett; first six miles Bartlett to Bemis Station completed by August 1874.
  • August 7, 1875 — First train on the mountain route arrives at Fabyan Station; railroad opened 1875, Portland through the notch to Fabyans.
  • 1895 — Frankenstein Trestle iron replaced with steel trusses.
  • 1898 — Willey House, enlarged as an inn after the tragedy, burns.
  • 1912–1913 — State buys the northern notch land for $62,000 in 1912 under a 1912 bill; most Crawford Notch land acquired 1913, almost 6,000 acres.
  • 1975–1977 — Crawford House closes 1975, contents auctioned 1976, burns November 20, 1977; site now AMC Highland Center.
  • 1994 — Crawford Path designated a National Recreation Trail; path now part of the Appalachian Trail from Mount Pierce to the summit.
