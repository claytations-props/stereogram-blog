Mauch Chunk, the town that sold coal, then sold the ride

Published October 24, 2026

Mauch Chunk was built to move coal. When a better coal route arrived, the old gravity railroad did not die. It became the attraction, and the town was marketed as the “Switzerland of America.” The railroad outlived its freight job by decades, then closed in the Depression. The town later lost even its name.

This is a research note, separate from the catalogue records. It does not change any catalogue title, place, date, or blurb. Photographs in this post are illustration only, with captions taken from the source catalogue.

Coal first

The Lehigh Coal and Navigation Company was formed in 1818 by Josiah White and Erskine Hazard. Its problem was moving coal from Summit Hill about nine miles to Mauch Chunk on the Lehigh River, where it could be loaded for Easton and Philadelphia.

Ground was broken for the Gravity Railroad on January 1, 1827, and the line from Summit Hill to Mauch Chunk was completed by May the same year. Loaded cars ran downhill by gravity at 96 feet per mile. Mules originally returned the empties.

That is from the National Register nomination for the Mauch Chunk and Summit Hill Switchback Railroad, entered June 3, 1976, which also lists the line as 8.5 miles between Summit Hill and Mauch Chunk, now Jim Thorpe.[1]

The switch to tourism

The National Canal Museum’s Switchback ephemera post puts the turn plainly:[2] by 1870, the railroad was no longer the main means of transporting coal, but it had become a popular tourist destination. Mauch Chunk had developed a reputation as the “Switzerland of America,” with settings described as ideal for tourists escaping dirty, crowded East Coast cities.

The nomination gives the mechanism behind that turn.[1] By 1872, the Hauto Tunnel provided a more efficient means for transporting coal. By 1874, the Central Railroad of New Jersey purchased the railroad and leased it to local residents who operated it for the many tourists who visited. The nomination’s summary is that Mauch Chunk became a tourist mecca for residents of the urban areas of the East Coast.

The 1921 season shows how routine that tourism had become. The Canal Museum describes a ticket for one trip,[2] a timetable with six excursions per day lasting just under 90 minutes, and trolley cars from the Carbon County Courthouse to the Switchback depot. Riders were hoisted to 1,500 feet at Mt. Pisgah, paused at High Point, rode six miles down, and were hoisted again over 1,600 feet to Summit Hill, where they could visit surface mining operations and the “burning mines” that caught fire in 1859.

The end, with dates kept straight

Do not collapse the end into one year. The sources found so far distinguish three steps:

  • Last cars ran in October 1933, according to Lehigh Gorge Scenic Railway history,[3] which also notes only 139 riders on Independence Day 1932.
  • The line was sold for scrap in 1937. Clio dates the purchase to September 2, 1937, by Isaac Weiner for $18,100.[4] The Switchback Gravity Railroad Foundation agrees it ceased operations in 1933 and its metal parts were sold for scrap in 1937.
  • The National Register nomination says by 1937 the line was abandoned,[1] primarily because the Central Railroad of New Jersey no longer ran excursions.

Wikipedia’s shorthand “operated until 1932” should not be used alone. It appears to mark a different endpoint than the last runs.

The name that disappeared

In 1954, the communities of Mauch Chunk and East Mauch Chunk merged to form the borough of Jim Thorpe, according to Britannica.[5] That basic fact is only half the story. The rename was part of a burial deal, and the name and the grave were tied together.

Jim Thorpe died on March 28, 1953, married to his third wife, Patricia Thorpe. He died intestate, and his estate was assigned to Patricia.[6] A plan reported in the Mauch Chunk Times-News on November 9, 1953, shows what was being proposed locally before the merger: a citizens committee, through Joseph L. Boyle, had communicated with Patricia Thorpe in Tulsa, seeking permission to bring Thorpe's body here for permanent burial. If voters approved, the twin boroughs would become a national shrine. The plan reported in that paper also called for a hospital for heart and cancer patients, to be financed nationally through a foundation program.[8]

The agreement that followed was dated May 19, 1954, between Patricia Thorpe and the Boroughs of Mauch Chunk and East Mauch Chunk. It set terms for the renaming of the municipalities and the interment of Jim Thorpe. As part of that agreement, the municipalities were consolidated under the name Jim Thorpe. It provided that Patricia Thorpe, her heirs, administrators and executors would not remove or cause to be removed the body of Jim Thorpe, and that obligation was binding only for so long as the boroughs are officially known and designated as Jim Thorpe.[6] Pursuant to that agreement, Thorpe's remains were interred within the Borough, on Borough-owned land in a mausoleum maintained by the Borough.[6]

That agreement was reached despite the objection of several of Thorpe's children, and Patricia Thorpe intended the Borough as the final and permanent resting place for her husband.[7] In October 2014, the Third Circuit threw out a ruling that would have allowed Thorpe's remains to be moved to American Indian land in Oklahoma, ensuring that, for the time being, the grave site would remain in the town that bears his name.[7]

So the town did not simply lose its old name and gain a new one. It traded Mauch Chunk for Jim Thorpe in a deal in which the new name was the condition that kept the burial in place. The hospital described in the November 1953 plan is not the memorial that stands today. What stands is the mausoleum described in the next section.

What survived

The Switchback itself did not survive as a railroad, but its route did. Pennsylvania's Department of Conservation and Natural Resources lists the Switchback Railroad Trail at 12.81 miles in Carbon County, made of two nine-mile routes that intersect. Riders start in Summit Hill and bike downhill toward Jim Thorpe, with the easier southern down track running along Mauch Chunk Lake.[9] DiscoverNEPA describes the same trail as a 12.8-mile trail connecting Jim Thorpe and Summit Hill, following the route of the Switchback Gravity Railroad.[10]

The Thorpe memorial also stands in Jim Thorpe, on North Street, Route 93. Clio describes a red granite mausoleum resting on mounds of soil from Thorpe's native Oklahoma, the New York Polo Grounds, and Stockholm's Olympic stadium, where Thorpe won gold medals in the decathlon and pentathlon in 1912, with a 2007 statue of Thorpe as a football player and a 2011 statue showing Thorpe with a discus.[11] A second visitor account agrees on the two statues and a large marble sarcophagus, on Route 903 north of town.[12] Thorpe, according to both Clio and the town's history, never set foot in the borough while alive.[11]

That is the strange ending to the coal to tourism story: the town that once sold a ride down a mountain now sells a walk and a bike ride along the same grade, and it carries the name of a man who never visited it, because his burial, not his life, brought the name there.

What this note does not claim

  • It does not claim Mauch Chunk was the second most popular tourist destination in America. That claim appears on a foundation site and has no independent source in the research log yet, so it is cut.
  • It does not date or retitle any catalog card.
