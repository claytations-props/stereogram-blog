Research Notes

Watkins Glen, where every waterfall got a name before it got a staircase

A newspaper editor named every fall in the gorge and sold tickets to see them. A painter came to paint one fall and stayed twenty years, and his son photographed the same falls. The names, the pictures, and the stone walk are what survive.

The gorge that had to be named

A creek runs out of the hills above Seneca Lake and drops through a gorge so narrow and steep that for most of its length there was nowhere to walk beside it. The village at its mouth had been called Jefferson, then Watkins, after Dr. Samuel Watkins, and the gorge itself was known locally as Big Gully. The Seneca town at the head of the lake, Catharine's Town, had been burned by the American army in September 1779.

A newspaper editor named Morvalden Ells, a resident of Watkins, decided the gorge could be sold. It belonged to George Freer, who had inherited it, and Ells went to him with a plan. He hired workmen to build wooden stairs and paths and put a gated entrance outside, and he gave every part of the gorge a name, in the order a walker meets them — Entrance Amphitheatre, Cavern Cascade, the Grotto, the Vista, Glen Cathedral, Glen of the Pools, the Artist's Dream, Pluto Fall, Glen Omega — and more besides. He announced through the press that on and after the 4th day of July, 1863, Watkins Glen would be open as a summer resort for visitors, and a claimant for a share of the favors bestowed upon Niagara and Saratoga. From 8,000 to 10,000 persons visited during the balance of that season, though only the stretch from the Entrance Amphitheatre to Cavern Cascade was open.

A walk in wool, hand on the stair

The guide book tells a visitor what to wear: it is frequently necessary to use the hand in climbing the stairways, and a long dress is inconvenient to manage. The dress should be of woolen material, for even in the mid-summer the glen is cool.

The cards show the walkers themselves: a couple resting on logs in the glen.

A little way in, under shelving cliffs, is the foot of the Long Staircase. Before climbing it, the visitor steps into a cavern, almost circular in form, dark and damp, called the Grotto, directly behind the sheet of water of Cavern Cascade. The rocky walls of the Grotto reverberate the echoes of the falling water until the sound is fairly deafening, and the light of the outer world gleaming through the transparent stream in front gives it the appearance of molten silver.

The staircase climbs to the Vista. At the top stands the Swiss Cottage, on the site of the little chalet Ells first built and called the Evergreen. It forms part of the Glen Mountain House. Horses could not be used to haul the timbers and lumber to their positions, and thousands of feet of the pathways, and many of the stairs, are cut in the solid rock.

The painter and his son

A few rods above the Mountain House, Captain James Hope erected an art gallery holding more than one hundred of his finest paintings, chief among them his picture of Rainbow Falls and his Army of the Potomac. Hope was a Scottish-born painter and Civil War veteran whose living from portraits had dried up after the war. He was paid ten thousand dollars to paint Rainbow Falls, and two years later he moved to Watkins Glen with his wife Julia and built his cottage and gallery-studio beside the glen. His gallery charged twenty-five cents to enter, a separate fee, because, as the guide says, it does not belong to the Glen.

He also stocked the gallery with souvenirs and stereoscopic cards of glen scenes made by his photographer son, James Douglas Hope, who sold the pictures to the tourists walking the stairs. A decade later Hope was doing the glen's seasonal repairs himself. He painted hundreds of glen scenes in the twenty years before his death in the village.

Rainbow Falls is the fall the whole enterprise points at. A little brook leaps over a cliff the guide puts at nearly four hundred feet high, and falls over a projecting shelf curved outward in a crescent, the water descending in a myriad of tiny threads and drops, forming a sparkling crystal veil, behind which our course leads. On a fair afternoon, a visitor looking through the veil beholds two most beautiful rainbows, a primary and secondary. Mark Twain, writing in 1872, offered Rainbow Fall as the example of scenery that stirs a visitor "that the tears almost come into his eyes so potent is the charm exerted" — and warned that the fall "would recede into pitiable insignificance if the callous tourist drew on arithmetic on it."

At Glen Cathedral the walls tower over three hundred feet by the guide's measure, the floor is smooth rock, and the Central Cascade forms the Choir. A visitor in the guide said he had often reflected upon the insignificance of man, but never so fully realized what a mere atom he was in this incomprehensible universe, as when standing in this vast Cathedral and looking up its towering walls. One summer a man and two women were hit by falling rock here, and one woman was killed.

The hotel burns, the state buys it

Freer sold the glen to E. B. Parsons for $25,000, and Ells stayed on as manager. Parsons sold it to John Lytle for $100,000, and Lytle built the Glen Mountain House, with a suspension bridge across the gorge. Andrew Haswell Green bought it for $100,000. Green was shot dead in a case of mistaken identity, and the Glen Mountain House burned a month later and was demolished. Ells died a year before the state bought the glen from Green's estate for just shy of $50,000, half what Green had paid — the one sale in the chain that was not about profit.

The American Scenic and Historic Preservation Society took over, and the reservation opened free. The state widened the paths, replaced wood with concrete, and cut the bridges from ten to three. The village of Watkins changed its name to Watkins Glen, because guide books, postcards, and people had been calling it that for years.

The flood, and the stone walk

The wooden walk did not survive the water. On July 7, 1935, thunderstorms dropped up to twelve inches of rain in three hours, a log jam in the glen was dislodged, and the rush of water destroyed many of the gorge's bridges, railings, and walkways. Gift shops at the mouth of the glen were destroyed, including one that housed J. D. Hope's works. More than forty people died in the region's flash floods. The Civilian Conservation Corps camp that had opened in the park that same year rebuilt the walk in stone, finished by park engineers in 1938.

The stone walk is the one visitors use now: nineteen waterfalls, more than eight hundred stone steps. The cards in the archive carry the names Ells gave the gorge, and the views J. D. Hope photographed are the views his father painted. The names on the signs are still the ones a newspaper editor invented to sell tickets, and the group posed among the rocks is the audience he sold them to.

Appendix

Timeline

  • 1779 — September: the American army burns Catharine's Town and its cornfields, in the Sullivan-Clinton campaign.
  • 1791–1794 — The Watkins-Flint purchase; John W. Watkins and his brother Charles arrive at the south end of Seneca Lake. The Watkins brothers build a sawmill, blacksmith, and gristmill in the gorge, then called Big Gully.
  • 1828 — Dr. Samuel Watkins arrives, builds flour, saw, and plaster mills in the gorge, damming it upstream.
  • 1851 — Samuel Watkins dies; his widow Cynthia remarries George Freer, who inherits the estate including the gorge. First railroad reaches the village.
  • 1852 — The village of Jefferson is renamed Watkins, for Dr. Samuel Watkins.
  • 1856 — Morvalden Ells, a newspaper editor from Elmira, moves to Watkins and buys the local paper.
  • 1858 — Ells hires workers to build wooden stairs, clear and widen paths, and build a gated entrance.
  • July 4, 1863 — The glen opens as Freer's Glen, with only the Entrance Amphitheatre to Cavern Cascade accessible. From 8,000 to 10,000 persons visit in the first season, by the guide's count; the marker records upwards of 10,000 tickets sold in the first year.
  • 1864 — The Long Staircase is built from Cavern Cascade to the top of the gorge; the trail to Glen Cathedral and to the Glen of Pools and Rainbow Falls opens in July.
  • 1865 — National recognition: journal articles around the United States begin publishing descriptions of the glen; the section beyond Rainbow Falls opens for the season.
  • 1869 — Freer sells the glen to E. B. Parsons for $25,000. Ells stays on as manager.
  • 1870 — Parsons replaces the old Glen Mountain House with a two-story Swiss Chalet, the building the 1886 guide calls the Swiss Cottage; James Hope is commissioned for $10,000 to paint Rainbow Falls.
  • 1872 — Hope moves to Watkins Glen with his wife Julia and builds his cottage and gallery-studio; his son James Douglas Hope photographs the glen in stereoscopic 3D and sells prints to tourists. Parsons sells the glen to John Lytle for $100,000. June 20: a man and two women are hit by falling rock in Glen Cathedral; one woman is killed.
  • 1873 — Lytle builds the grand Glen Mountain House, housing 300 guests, with a suspension bridge linking the two sides of the glen.
  • 1882 — Hope is doing the glen's seasonal repairs and ornamental structures, and working with the Glen Mountain House souvenir business.
  • 1892 — October 20: James Hope dies at his home in Watkins Glen. His family keeps the gallery open.
  • 1902 — The New York Central Railroad begins selling Sunday excursion tickets to the glen from New York City; campers multiply and hotel business falls.
  • 1904 — A New York auction catalogue lists 83 of Hope's paintings, including the Antietam panoramas, which are later conserved by the National Park Service and shown at Antietam National Battlefield.
  • 1903 — November 13: Andrew Haswell Green is fatally shot in a case of mistaken identity. December: the Glen Mountain House burns and is demolished.
  • 1905 — Morvalden Ells dies; he is buried in Glenwood Cemetery.
  • 1906 — New York State purchases the glen from Green's estate for just shy of $50,000, half his purchase price; the American Scenic and Historic Preservation Society takes over management.
  • 1907 — Watkins Glen State Reservation opens to visitors free of charge.
  • 1908 — The ASHPS removes the Swiss Chalet; the Entrance Tunnel is carved out of the cliffs and the Spiral Tunnel excavated.
  • 1911 — Management passes to the newly formed Watkins Glen Commission, after a scandal over construction funds.
  • 1924 — The Finger Lakes Commission of the New York State Council of Parks takes over management.
  • 1926 — The village of Watkins formally becomes Watkins Glen.
  • 1935 — Camp SP-44 of the Civilian Conservation Corps opens in the western end of the park. July 7: thunderstorms drop up to 12 inches of rain in 3 hours; the flood destroys the gorge's bridges, railings, and walkways and takes out the railroad trestle; more than 40 people die in the region's flash floods. The CCC rebuilds the walk in stone.
  • 1938 — Park engineers complete the stone rebuild of the gorge walkways, out of low, mortared stone walls and arches.
  • 1941 — The CCC troop is disbanded; its camp, Hidden Valley, is turned over to the local 4-H club.
